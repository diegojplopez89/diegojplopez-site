About

My work has always lived between people and systems

I was born in Nogales and raised in Tucson, and most of my professional life has been shaped by the borderlands. I have spent more than a decade working in humanitarian response, nonprofit leadership, public administration, migration, public health, clinical social work, and the practical systems organizations use to serve people.

I started close to the work

My path into leadership was not a straight jump into an executive role. I started in frontline and volunteer work and moved through program coordination, management, director-level responsibilities, and eventually executive leadership. That progression matters to me because I learned the work from several levels of the organization.

At Casa Alitas, I ultimately led a $12 million multi-site humanitarian reception system serving up to 1,800 asylum seekers nightly with 62 staff and more than 600 volunteers. During the busiest periods, the challenge was not simply sheltering people. It was making staffing, transportation, partnerships, funding, data, volunteer systems, communication, and basic human care work together at the same time.

What I value most from that experience is the relationships

Large humanitarian systems only work when people trust one another. At the border, that meant county and federal partners, nonprofits, faith communities, volunteers, funders, transportation providers, staff, and the people we were receiving all had to be part of the same response. I learned that scale is important, but relationships are what make scale possible.

Systems became part of the job because they had to

I did not enter human services intending to become a data-systems person. That grew out of necessity. When the work became too complex for informal processes, I started building forms, dashboards, referral workflows, reporting structures, and shared data practices that helped people coordinate more reliably.

Later work with Catholic Charities USA and Cherokee Federal expanded that experience into multi-agency referral systems, high-volume data, national capacity dashboards, operational reporting, and service-planning tools. More recently, I have been experimenting with local-first AI, automation, and knowledge systems. I am interested in technology when it solves a real problem, not simply because it is new.

My LMSW adds another perspective

I am now a Licensed Master Social Worker in Arizona and practice clinically in Tucson. I do not see that as a completely separate career. Direct practice gives me a different view of the same questions I have worked on at the organizational level: trauma, access, family systems, service coordination, and what happens when a policy or process reaches a real person.
