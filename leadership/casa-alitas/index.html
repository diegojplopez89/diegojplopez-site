Casa Alitas · Humanitarian Leadership

A public record of humanitarian work at Casa Alitas

Casa Alitas was a central part of Diego Piña Lopez's humanitarian career in Tucson. Independent reporting over several years documents his work as the program grew from a small volunteer-led shelter response into a large, multi-site system serving migrant and asylum-seeking families.

Growing a humanitarian response under pressure

NPR documented Casa Alitas' rapid expansion in 2019 and identified Diego Javier Piña Lopez as the shelter's lead coordinator. In that reporting, he described the program's progression from helping asylum-seeking families at Tucson's bus station to operating larger shelter sites, including the rapid opening of the former Benedictine Monastery.

Independent documentation of organizational scale

AZ Luminaria's February 2024 reporting provides one of the clearest public records of the scale Casa Alitas reached. The story describes Diego as director and contrasts the program's 2019 structure—when he was its only paid staff member and the shelter served roughly 30 people per day—with an operation supported by approximately 40–50 staff, nearly 900 volunteers, multiple locations, and a central facility coordinating intake, food, medical services, lodging, transportation, and departures.

The same report documents days when the organization served hundreds and sometimes more than 1,000 people, placing the leadership role in a concrete operational context rather than reducing it to a job title.

Leadership as migration patterns changed

By 2023, Cronkite News identified Diego as associate director of Casa Alitas while reporting on changing border encounter patterns. The coverage placed his role in the context of shelter capacity, seasonal migration patterns, and the operational consequences of changes across the Tucson Sector.

Working with government and community partners

Diocese of Tucson coverage of Governor Katie Hobbs' visit to Casa Alitas in 2023 identified Diego as part of the program's leadership team and quoted him on continuing the work, serving arriving families, and improving the response. The visit reflected the kind of cross-sector coordination that became necessary as shelter operations expanded.

Communicating the human impact of funding decisions

In early 2024, local reporting repeatedly turned to Diego for operational context as federal shelter funding approached a cliff. AZPM reported his estimates for the basic costs of feeding, clothing, and sheltering guests and his concern that families with very young children could be released into public spaces if reception capacity disappeared.

KJZZ separately reported that Diego had spent a decade working with migrant and asylum-seeking communities in Tucson and quoted him on shelter volumes, street-release risks, and the downstream consequences of losing organized reception capacity.

Work that connected operations, data, and people

The Casa Alitas role required more than managing shelter space. Diego's broader professional record includes humanitarian operations, staffing and volunteer coordination, transportation, partnerships, public communication, data systems, referral workflows, and implementation work across organizations. Those experiences later informed work in public health, research, systems implementation, and clinical social work.
